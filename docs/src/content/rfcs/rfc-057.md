## Decision

A question to the law can be about a place. The caller gives a shape, and the law says the rest.

The law format gets a value type, an operation, and two fields on an open term.

### A shape and OVERLAPS

A `shape` is a geometry: a point, a line or a surface. Its value is text in WKT. A shape carries no coordinate system, so the two shapes in one operation must be in the same one. Dutch regulations publish their geometry in the RD system (EPSG:28992), in meters, and the examples here use it.

`OVERLAPS` takes two shapes and gives a boolean. It holds when the interiors of the two shapes meet.

| The location | The area | `OVERLAPS` |
|---|---|---|
| a surface half on it | a surface | true |
| a surface against its edge | a surface | false |
| a point inside it | a surface | true |
| a point on its boundary | a surface | false |

An area is the output of the provision that designates it. Another provision reads it the way it reads any other value, also from another rulework.

```yaml
articles:
  - number: '1.2'
    text: |
      Als beperkingengebied waterkering wordt aangewezen het gebied dat als
      zodanig is aangegeven in bijlage 1.
    machine_readable:
      execution:
        output:
          - name: beperkingengebied_waterkering
            type: shape
        actions:
          - output: beperkingengebied_waterkering
            value: 'POLYGON((154000 463600, 157000 463600, 157000 463650, 154000 463650, 154000 463600))'

  - number: '3.1'
    text: |
      Het is verboden zonder omgevingsvergunning een bouwactiviteit te
      verrichten in het beperkingengebied waterkering.
    machine_readable:
      execution:
        parameters:
          - name: location
            type: shape
        input:
          - name: beperkingengebied_waterkering
            type: shape
            source:
              output: beperkingengebied_waterkering
        output:
          - name: vergunning_vereist
            type: boolean
        actions:
          - output: vergunning_vereist
            value:
              operation: OVERLAPS
              subject: $location
              value: $beperkingengebied_waterkering
```

The regulation in this example is fictional.

In the Dutch reading of an action tree ([RFC-054](/rfcs/rfc-054)) the operation reads as "[a] overlapt [b]".

### The place decides which regulation speaks

An open term may name a `location` and a `territory`. The territory is an output of a law that gives the territory of an authority. A regulation that carries the code of an authority then fills the term only when the location overlaps the territory of that authority.

An open term may say `combine: OR`. Every regulation in scope is then asked, article by article, and the term holds when one of them says so.

```yaml
open_terms:
  - id: omgevingsplan_verbiedt_zonder_vergunning
    type: boolean
    delegated_to: gemeenteraad
    delegation_type: GEMEENTELIJKE_VERORDENING
    location: $location
    territory:
      regulation: kadasterwet
      output: grens_van_gemeente
    combine: OR
```

The territory of a municipality is in no regulation of that municipality. The register that keeps it has a law of its own, and that law is a rulework like any other. Its article reads the register and gives the boundary:

```yaml
$id: kadasterwet
articles:
  - number: '48'
    text: |
      3. [...] De landelijke kadastrale kaart bevat: [...]
      c. de rijksgrens en de grens van een provincie of gemeente;
    machine_readable:
      execution:
        parameters:
          - name: gemeente_code
            type: string
        input:
          - name: grens_van_gemeente
            type: shape
            source: {}
        output:
          - name: grens_van_gemeente
            type: shape
        actions:
          - output: grens_van_gemeente
            value: $grens_van_gemeente
```

The engine asks that output on the calculation date, once for each code a candidate regulation carries. It passes the code under the name of the field that holds it, here `gemeente_code`, and nothing else of the case.

```gherkin
Scenario: 300 m2 of bijgebouwen in the area Wonen
  Given the calculation date is "2026-11-17"
  Given the following "basisregistratie_kadaster" data with key "gemeente_code" for law "kadasterwet":
    | gemeente_code | grens_van_gemeente                               |
    | GM0273        | MULTIPOLYGON(((166920.623 468329.737, ...)))     |
  Given parameter "location" is "POLYGON((167795 472320, 167801 472320, 167801 472330, 167795 472330, 167795 472320))"
  When I evaluate "vergunningplicht_omgevingsplanactiviteit" of "omgevingswet"
  Then output "vergunningplicht_omgevingsplanactiviteit" is true
```

The scenario names no municipality. That the location lies in Putten follows from the two shapes. Every step in it is one the grammar already has.

What a term with `combine: OR` gives. Every article in scope is asked, and the first row that fits decides:

| The articles in scope | The term |
|---|---|
| one says true | true |
| one fails | that failure |
| one cannot be translated | untranslatable |
| one cannot decide for lack of a fact, or the territory of a regulation is unknown | unknown |
| every one is silent or says false | false |
| there is none | the default of the term, or an error when it has none |

True comes first, so a defect in the plan of the neighboring municipality does not break a case that the own plan already decides. The failure is recorded.

What a term with a `location` does:

- A regulation without a territory, such as a national one, is in scope wherever the location is. So are the regulation that declares the term and a regulation of the same territory as that one.
- A location that is unknown makes the term unknown.
- A territory that is unknown, because the register holds nothing for the code, leaves it open whether that regulation is in scope. The term is then unknown, also when it has a default. With `combine: OR` an article of a regulation that is in scope can still make it true.
- A `territory` that names a law that is not loaded, or an output that is not a shape, is an error. So is a regulation that carries more than one code.
- A point on the boundary between two territories lies in neither.
- A location across two territories brings the regulations of both in scope. With `combine: OR` both are asked. Without it they are two candidates of the same layer, which the engine refuses as ambiguous.

`combine` is for a boolean term, and not for a term an authority decides per case.

The engine records what it did. For each term with a `location` or `combine` the result, the receipt and the trace hold the location, each candidate regulation with its code, where its territory came from, a hash of that territory and whether the location overlapped it, and each article that was asked with what it gave.

The engine refuses a shape that is not WKT, has a third dimension, describes an invalid or empty geometry, is a collection of different kinds, or has a coordinate beyond 1e12. A refused shape is an error and not false. Two shapes cannot be compared with `EQUALS` or `IN`, which would compare their text. A shape is not allowed as a column or as the type of an open term.

## Context

Part of the law speaks about places. A water board forbids building in a strip along a dike, and an omgevingsplan sets a norm for one plot. Such a rule names an area, and the geometry of that area is adopted together with the rule.

The format had no way to say this. A rule could depend on a place only when its text named something the format could hold, such as a range of postcodes.

Which regulation applies depends on the place as well. The engine picked a municipal regulation by a `gemeente_code` that the caller passed, so the caller had to know the answer to the first question the law asks.

## Why

### A shape is written out in the rulework

The value of an area is the geometry itself, the way the value of a date is the date. A version of a rulework then holds everything it says, and a changed boundary is a new version. A scenario writes a location in the same notation.

A regulation published under the Omgevingswet does not contain its geometry. It names each area and gives an identifier, and the coordinates are published as a separate object. The shape in a rulework is therefore a transcription of that object. The identifier belongs in the text of the provision that lists the areas, so the shape can be checked against its source.

### Interiors, not boundaries

Two areas that border each other share a boundary. With `OVERLAPS` a building that stands against that boundary lies in one of them, and a building across it lies in both. The same holds for two municipalities.

Whether a rule means "partly in the area" or "wholly in the area" is a reading of that rule. `OVERLAPS` expresses the first. This RFC adds no operation for the second.

### The territory comes from the law that keeps it

The boundary of a municipality is laid down by a herindelingsregeling: a law, or for a small correction a decision of the councils or of provinciale staten (Wet algemene regels herindeling, article 3). Gedeputeerde staten then describe the boundary "met behulp van kadastrale kenmerken en indien dat niet mogelijk is met behulp van coördinaten van het verschoven stelsel van rijksdriehoeksmeting" (article 2). The Basisregistratie Kadaster keeps the result: its national cadastral map contains "de grens van een provincie of gemeente" (Kadasterwet, article 48 lid 3 onder c), in a net of points in the RD system (article 52).

So the law names a place where the boundary is found, and the rulework of that law gives it. The corpus already does this for a person: the Wet basisregistratie personen reads the register, and the Kieswet reads that law.

That a plan holds in the territory of its municipality is in the Omgevingswet: "De gemeenteraad stelt voor het gehele grondgebied van de gemeente één omgevingsplan vast" (article 2.4). The open term that the plan fills names the Kadasterwet as the source of that territory.

The boundary on a date is the one the register holds on that date. The rulework of the Kadasterwet is asked on the calculation date like any other.

### One article is enough, and no plan is not no ban

The Omgevingswet speaks of an activity "waarvoor in het omgevingsplan is bepaald dat het is verboden deze zonder omgevingsvergunning te verrichten". A plan says such things article by article, one for building and one for felling a tree. The term holds when one article says so.

With `combine: OR` three outcomes stay apart. A plan with an article that forbids the activity gives true. A plan in which no article forbids it gives false. No plan for that place gives no answer, and for a term without a default the engine says so.

Without `combine` a term takes one regulation, as before.

### What was tried on real law

Part of the Omgevingsplan gemeente Putten was modelled, with the published geometry of the areas at one address and the boundary of the municipality from the register. Five ruleworks are involved: the Omgevingswet, the Besluit bouwwerken leefomgeving, the Kadasterwet, the plan, and the bestemmingsplan of 2018 that is part of it.

The plan designates its areas in an annex, so there is a provision that gives each area as its output. A norm that holds in one area needs nothing new: the number is in the text of the article, and the area only says where it holds. The three areas in the rulework have five points each, which reads well.

Two articles of the plan fill the same term of the Omgevingswet, one for building and one for breeding maggots. The scenarios name no municipality.

### Alternatives considered

**The caller names the areas.** The question arrives with a list of the areas the location lies in, and a rule tests that list with `IN`. The format would not change. The spatial test then happens before execution, where no trace or receipt records it, and a scenario states the outcome of the test it is supposed to check.

**A reference to a geometry file as the value.** A path is a pointer and not the value. A version of a rulework would depend on a file beside it that can change without the version changing.

**A key on the article that names where it applies.** This reads well for a rule that holds in one area. It cannot express an area in the middle of a rule, such as an exception that does not hold in a protected townscape.

**`IN` for shapes.** A reader takes "in" to mean wholly inside, and the operation would get a second meaning that depends on the type of its operands.

**The territory in the rulework.** A plan would carry the boundary of its municipality. That boundary is not adopted with the plan, and a boundary change would then require a new version of every regulation of that municipality.

**The caller hands the territories to the engine.** A first version did this, with a step of its own in the scenario language and a method of its own on the engine. It worked, and it put a fact beside the law format that no provision in the corpus stood behind. The register of persons does not reach the engine that way either.

## Not decided here

- **Wholly inside, and a share of a surface.** Rules such as "geheel gelegen in" or "voor meer dan de helft" need another operation.
- **Distance.** "Binnen 50 m van" needs a derived shape or an operation on distance.
- **Large shapes in a rulework.** The boundary of the municipality of Putten has 2,185 points. As a territory it is a fact from the register and stays out of the rulework. An area of that size designated by a regulation would not be readable when written out, and needs another way to be carried.
- **The act that laid a boundary down.** A herindelingsregeling and the description of the boundary by gedeputeerde staten are not modelled. The rulework of the Kadasterwet gives what the register holds.
- **The territory of a water board or a province.** The example covers a municipality. The Kadasterwet also keeps the boundary of a province; where the area of a water board is laid down was not looked up.
- **One answer to "may I do this here?"** The Omgevingswet has several bans and no provision that adds them up. A scenario asks one ban of the law. An answer over all of them belongs to a service that sits on top of the law.
