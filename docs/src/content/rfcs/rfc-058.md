## Context

Awb article 4:13 sets the period within which an administrative body decides on an application:

> 1. Een beschikking dient te worden gegeven binnen de bij wettelijk voorschrift bepaalde termijn of, bij het ontbreken van zulk een termijn, binnen een redelijke termijn na ontvangst van de aanvraag.
> 2. De redelijke termijn is in ieder geval verstreken wanneer het bestuursorgaan binnen acht weken na ontvangst van de aanvraag geen beschikking heeft gegeven.

In the law format, 4:13 is a hook ([RFC-007](/rfcs/rfc-007)): it fires on every `BESCHIKKING`, whichever law gives it. Paragraph 1 leaves the period to the special law, and paragraph 2 is the fallback. That is the shape of an open term ([RFC-003](/rfcs/rfc-003)): 4:13 declares `wettelijke_beslistermijn` with a default of eight weeks after receipt, and a special law that sets its own period fills the term with `implements`.

Take a fictional *Subsidiewet voorbeeld*. Its minister decides on an application within thirteen weeks, or twenty-six for a subsidy year before 2026:

```yaml
# algemene_wet_voorbeeld, article 4:13
machine_readable:
  hooks:
    - hook_point: post_actions
      applies_to: {legal_character: BESCHIKKING, stage: BESLUIT}
  open_terms:
    - id: wettelijke_beslistermijn
      type: date
      required: false
      default:
        actions:
          - output: wettelijke_beslistermijn
            value: {operation: DATE_ADD, date: $datum_ontvangst, weeks: 8}
  execution:
    parameters:
      - {name: datum_ontvangst, type: date, required: true}
    output:
      - {name: uiterste_beslisdag, type: date}
    actions:
      - output: uiterste_beslisdag
        value: $wettelijke_beslistermijn
```

```yaml
# subsidiewet_voorbeeld, article 2
machine_readable:
  implements:
    - law: algemene_wet_voorbeeld
      article: '4:13'
      open_term: wettelijke_beslistermijn
  execution:
    parameters:
      - {name: subsidiejaar, type: number, required: true}
      - {name: datum_ontvangst, type: date, required: true}
    output:
      - {name: wettelijke_beslistermijn, type: date}
    actions:
      - output: wettelijke_beslistermijn
        value:
          operation: IF
          cases:
            - when: {operation: LESS_THAN, subject: $subsidiejaar, value: 2026}
              then: {operation: DATE_ADD, date: $datum_ontvangst, weeks: 26}
          default: {operation: DATE_ADD, date: $datum_ontvangst, weeks: 13}
```

Before this RFC the engine could not run this pair, for two reasons.

The parameters were filtered twice. A hook receives only the parameters its own article declares, so 4:13 got `datum_ontvangst` and nothing else. The implementing article then received the parameters it declares, chosen from what 4:13 had. `subsidiejaar` was gone by then, and a subsidy decision failed with `Variable not found: subsidiejaar`. The filtering is deliberate. It is least privilege: a hook or an implementation sees what it declares and not, say, the BSN of the case that happened to call it.

The second reason is that `implements` knows no host. The implements index is keyed on the declaring law, article and term, and `find_implementations` filters on territorial scope, the delegation gate and validity on the date. Nothing in that lookup asks which decision the hook fires on. The *Subsidiewet voorbeeld* would therefore fill the decision period of every `BESCHIKKING` in the corpus, a building permit included.

The workaround in use today has 4:13 read an output such as `uiterste_beslisdag` by name from the outputs of the law that gave the decision. A host that names the output differently gets eight weeks, and nothing says so.

## Decision

**An open term of an article that fires as a hook is filled only by the host law: the law of the article whose decision the hook fires on. That implementation receives its parameters from the host's scope.**

In detail:

- **Host.** The host is the law of the article that fires the hook. Its scope is what that article hands its hooks: its parameters, the findings given for the execution, and its resolved inputs. For `post_actions` it also holds the article's outputs and those of the `pre_actions` hooks.
- **Candidates.** An implementation from any other law is no candidate. It leaves no record in the trace or the receipt, because it was never offered for this decision. The host needs no territorial scope check, just as the declaring law needs none: it is the law that applies to the case.
- **Parameters.** The host's implementing article receives the parameters it declares, chosen from the host's scope. The hook article itself still receives only what it declares, and its `default` reads those.
- **Delegation gate.** The gate and the version filter apply to the host as they apply to any candidate. A host whose `regulatory_layer` is not the `delegation_type` of the term is refused with a `DelegationRefusal` in the trace and the receipt, and the default applies.
- **Outside a hook.** An article that runs because something called it, rather than because it fired as a hook, resolves its open terms as before. While the hook runs, however, every route that reaches the hook article belongs to the host's decision. A host that states its period in terms of the general rule reads 4:13 without its own filling, and that read gets the default, not the filling of another law.

## Why

### Benefits

The outcome follows from the chain being executed. Under the old lookup, a law or policy anywhere in the corpus that fills an open term of an Awb hook would hold for every decision the hook fires on, also in chains it has nothing to do with. Loading one more rulework could then change the decision period of an unrelated permit, and no reader of that permit's chain would see why. Restricting the filling to the host makes the result predictable from the chain alone.

A foreign implementation also lacks the context it needs. It does not know which parameters a given chain has, and it has no access to them. The *Subsidiewet voorbeeld* needs `subsidiejaar`; a building permit has no such thing. The host has that context, and the data is its own.

Least privilege stays intact. The case data goes from the host to the host's own article and no further, so it never leaves the law it came from. The hook does not receive it, and neither does any other law.

### Tradeoffs

- A filling that should hold for every decision, from a law other than the host, is no longer possible under a hook. No such rulework exists in the corpus today. If one appears, the default of the general law, or an override, is where it belongs.
- The declaring law's own articles are not the host either, so under a hook they no longer fill the term. Again, nothing in the corpus does this.
- Who the host is depends on who fires the hook. A decision given by a regulation under a framework law has that regulation as its host, and the framework law cannot fill the term. See the open questions.

### Alternatives Considered

**A slot on the open term naming the case data that goes through.** 4:13 would list, say, `passes: [subsidiejaar]`. That puts the parameters of laws the Awb does not know into the Awb, which reverses the direction that makes inversion of control work. It also leaves the second reason standing: the foreign filling would still apply to every decision.

**Forward all of the host's parameters to every implementation.** This removes the `Variable not found` error and drops least privilege with it. A regulation anywhere in the corpus would receive the full case of any decision the hook fires on, and it would still fill the term for all of them.

**Keep reading the host's output by name (the current workaround).** It rests on a naming convention, and a mismatch shows up in neither the trace nor the receipt.

**An override of the output of 4:13.** The host could replace `uiterste_beslisdag` with `overrides`. That models a departure (*"in afwijking van"*), while paragraph 1 delegates: the special law does not depart from 4:13, 4:13 points at it. The override would also replace the whole output, and the receipt would record a departure where the law describes a delegation.

### Implementation Notes

The prototype in this RFC's pull request covers the decision above.

- **Where the host comes from.** Before this change, the resolution context did not hold the host. `contextual_law_id` comes closest, but it names the law that started the execution chain, and that is not the host when a hook fires deeper in the chain. The host's parameters were not there at all. `fire_hooks` now records the host (hook article, host law, the parameters it hands its hooks) in the resolution context for as long as the hook article runs, and restores the previous value afterwards, also on an error.
- **Which article reads it.** Only the open terms of the hook article itself read the host. An article the hook calls, an implementing article and an override are no hook and see nothing of it. The hook article reached again during the hook run, by a base read or any other call, does read the host. The output cache is keyed without the host, so nothing a call computes while a hook runs goes into it.
- **The lookup.** `RuleResolver::find_host_implementations` restricts the implements index to the host law, without the scope check for it. The engine uses it for resolving the term, for deciding whether a term judged per case is filled (`filled_terms`), and for noting the findings of a policy that fills one (`note_policy_findings`).
- **Existing rulework.** No article in `corpus/regulation/`, `corpus/demo/`, `corpus-poc/` or the BDD features both fires as a hook and declares an open term; the only one is a schema conformance fixture, which is never executed. No rulework leans on the old behavior, and the full engine test suite passes unchanged.
- **Not covered.** `decision_terms`, which lists the open terms a decision may need for a form, does not walk hooks and is unchanged. No test yet pins the host lookup for a term judged per case (`decided_per_case_by`) on a hook, nor the exclusion from the output cache.

Tests in `packages/engine/tests/hook_host_implements.rs` pin the rule on fictional rulework. The host fills the term with a parameter of its own, and a decision of another law gets the default while a foreign filling does not count, also one that could run on the hook's own parameters. Called directly, the article behaves as before. A host that reads the general rule gets the default in that read, a municipal host fills the term without a `gemeente_code` among the hook's parameters, and the delegation gate still refuses a host of the wrong layer.

## Open questions

### Policy of the executing authority under the host

An executing authority can write its own policy that sets the decision period, for instance a beleidsregel under the *Subsidiewet voorbeeld*. Whether such a policy may set a period that 4:13 paragraph 1 reserves for a statutory provision is a legal question, and the answer for 4:13 is probably no. The engine should still be able to express the case. Under this RFC the policy is not the host, so it fills nothing under the hook. How would the engine know that it belongs to the host?

What makes this hard is that the format holds no reliable link from a regulation to the law it falls under:

- A regulation can state its grondslag in the top-level `legal_basis` (`law_id` and `article`). The engine parses it but does not use it, and in the corpus three of 42 versions carry it. For a beleidsregel the grondslag is moreover often Awb 4:81 itself, the general power to make policy, rather than the special law the policy executes.
- `shares_territory` links regulations with the same scope fields set to the same values. It says that two municipal regulations apply in the same place, not that one rests on the other, and two national regulations never share a territory.
- `implements` on an open term of the host links a policy to the host, but only where the host has declared such a term.
- `competent_authority` names who executes. One authority executes many laws, so it cannot tell which one a policy belongs to.

Several directions are open, and this RFC chooses none. The engine could follow `legal_basis` once it is filled and structured enough, with the transitive case (a ministeriële regeling under an AMvB under the act) still to be decided. The host could declare an open term of its own for the period and fill 4:13's term from it; a policy that `implements` that term then reaches the hook through the host, which the prototype already supports. Or a new link could be added to the format.

Which layer may fill the term stays a separate question from which law may. The delegation gate answers it through `delegation_type` on the open term, and it applies to the host now and would apply to whatever belongs to the host later. Establishing that a beleidsregel belongs to the host would not let it past a term that demands a statutory provision. It would be refused with a `DelegationRefusal` in the trace and the receipt, not passed over in silence.

### One layer for "wettelijk voorschrift"

`delegation_type` names one regulatory layer. "Bij wettelijk voorschrift bepaald" in 4:13 admits several (a wet, an AMvB, a ministeriële regeling, a verordening) and excludes a beleidsregel. The format cannot say that yet, so the fictional 4:13 in the tests declares no `delegation_type`.
