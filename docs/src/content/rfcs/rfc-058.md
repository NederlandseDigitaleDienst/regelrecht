## Context

Awb article 4:13 sets the period within which an administrative body decides on an application:

> 1. Een beschikking dient te worden gegeven binnen de bij wettelijk voorschrift bepaalde termijn of, bij het ontbreken van zulk een termijn, binnen een redelijke termijn na ontvangst van de aanvraag.
> 2. De redelijke termijn is in ieder geval verstreken wanneer het bestuursorgaan binnen acht weken na ontvangst van de aanvraag geen beschikking heeft gegeven.

Paragraph 1 leaves the period to the special law, and paragraph 2 is the fallback. That is the shape of an open term ([RFC-003](/rfcs/rfc-003)) on a hook ([RFC-007](/rfcs/rfc-007)). 4:13 fires on every `BESCHIKKING`, whichever law gives it, and declares `wettelijke_beslistermijn` with a default of eight weeks after receipt. A special law that sets its own period fills the term with `implements`.

Take a fictional *Subsidiewet voorbeeld* with two decisions that both fall under 4:13. Article 1 grants a subsidy, and its period depends on the subsidy year. Article 3 settles it after a report, and its period depends on the number of projects. Each decision has a parameter the other lacks, and 4:13 knows neither.

Two problems stood in the way.

The parameters are filtered twice. A hook receives only the parameters its own article declares, so 4:13 gets `datum_ontvangst` and nothing else. An implementing article then receives the parameters it declares, chosen from what 4:13 has. `subsidiejaar` is gone by then, and the grant fails with `Variable not found: subsidiejaar`. The filtering is deliberate. It is least privilege: a hook or an implementation sees what it declares and not, say, the BSN of the case that happened to call it.

The second problem is that `implements` knows nothing of the firing. The implements index is keyed on the declaring law, article and term. Nothing in the lookup asks which article the hook fires on, so a filling meant for the grant would also run for the settlement, and for a building permit under another law.

A first version of this RFC answered both problems with the law of the decision, the host law. Under a hook only the host law filled the term, with the host's parameters. That was too coarse in one direction and too strict in the other. Within one law, the grant and the settlement both fire 4:13 with different parameters, and a filling keyed on the law cannot tell them apart. And every filling from outside the host law stopped counting under a hook. That included a policy rule (*beleidsregel*) whose own hook article had its open term filled by another article of the same policy rule. Such bindings fell back to the default without a word. Execution policy of an administrative body also needs to fill a hook's term in practice, for instance where policy decides whether a decision is taken under mandate (Awb 10:10).

## Decision

**A filling of an open term of a hook article may name the article the hook fires on, its host. With a host, the filling applies only while the hook fires on that article, and it receives its parameters from that article's scope. Without a host, the filling fills the term wherever the article runs, as before, with the parameters of the hook article itself. A filling that names the host goes before one that names none.**

```yaml
# subsidiewet_voorbeeld, article 2
machine_readable:
  implements:
    - law: algemene_wet_voorbeeld
      article: '4:13'
      open_term: wettelijke_beslistermijn
      host:
        law: subsidiewet_voorbeeld
        article: '1'
```

In detail:

- **The field.** `host` on an `implements` entry is an object with `law` (an `$id`) and `article`, the same pair `implements` uses for the article that declares the term. It arrived with schema v0.8.24; a file on an earlier version that uses it is refused by the schema and by the loader.
- **The host.** The host is the article the hook fires on: the article that establishes the application for a hook on a submission, the article that gives the decision for a hook on a legal character. Its scope is what that article hands its hooks: its parameters, the findings given for the execution, and its resolved inputs. An input that only a filling naming the host reads is resolved before the hooks fire, because the engine resolves inputs only when something reads them ([RFC-043](/rfcs/rfc-043)). For `post_actions` the scope also holds the article's outputs and those of the `pre_actions` hooks.
- **With a host.** The filling is a candidate only while the hook fires on the article it names. It receives the parameters it declares, chosen from the host's scope. It may come from any regulation: the law of the host, a policy rule, a regulation of another law. Outside that firing it is no candidate and leaves no record, because it was never offered. Called directly, outside any hook, the hook article never gets it.
- **Without a host.** The filling is a candidate under every hook and outside one, exactly as an open term is filled anywhere else. It receives the parameters it declares, chosen from the hook article's own parameters. A parameter that only the host has is not there. The implementation then fails with `VariableNotFound`, the error any implementation gets for a required parameter that is not in its scope, and an optional one is unknown ([RFC-036](/rfcs/rfc-036)). It does not quietly get a value.
- **Precedence.** Under a hook the fillings that name the host come first, ranked among themselves, and then the fillings without a host, ranked among themselves. Within each group a conflict is resolved as for any open term: lex superior, then lex posterior, and an error when neither decides. The first filling in that order that gives a value fills the term. A filling that is silent for the case (null) passes the term on to the next. So does one held back because it reads the law it fills, and in the end the default applies.
- **Scope.** A filling that names the host is checked against the host's scope. The declaring law, the law of the host and a regulation of the territory of either need no scope check: the host is the article that applies to the case. A filling without a host is checked against the hook article's parameters, as it is outside a hook.
- **Delegation gate.** The gate and the version filter apply to both groups. A candidate whose `regulatory_layer` is not the `delegation_type` of the term is refused with a `DelegationRefusal` in the trace and the receipt. A policy rule that names the host is refused where the term demands a law, and the next candidate or the default applies.
- **Which article reads the host.** While a hook runs, every route that reaches an article firing as a hook on the same decision belongs to that decision. A hook that reads the output of another hook on it gets that decision's fillings. Another article of the hook law that is not itself a hook on the decision resolves its open terms as outside a hook.

## Why

### Benefits

The article is the unit that carries context. Each decision of a law has its own parameters, and the filling that needs them now says which decision it belongs to. The grant and the settlement of the *Subsidiewet voorbeeld* each get their own period, from their own data, under the same hook.

The context comes only where it is named. A filling without a host gets nothing it did not get before this RFC, and a filling that names a host gets the scope of exactly that article. Least privilege stays intact: the case data goes from the host to a filling that names it, and the hook and every other article still see only what they declare.

Fillings without a host work as they did before this RFC, and as an open term works outside a hook. A policy rule whose own article fills the term of its own hook article counts again, and so does a policy rule that fills the term of a hook article of a general law. The hook article run under a hook and the same article called directly give the same value, unless a filling names that firing.

Who may fill is the delegation gate's question again, not a side effect of who fires the hook. The law of the host, a policy rule and another regulation all go through the same gate, so execution policy can fill a term where the law leaves it to policy, and is refused where the law demands a statutory provision.

### Tradeoffs

- **A filling without a host applies everywhere the hook fires.** A regulation that fills the term without naming a host changes the outcome for every decision the hook fires on. That is the meaning of an `implements` without a host, and the receipt names the regulation that filled the term. The first version of this RFC prevented it by letting only the host law fill; that also stopped the fillings that were meant to apply everywhere, which is the larger loss.
- **One more field to get right.** A filling that needs the host's data and forgets `host` fails with `VariableNotFound` under the hook, and so points at the omission. A filling that names a host by a wrong article number never applies, and the default does. The loader cannot catch that, because the host may be in another file, loaded later.
- **A host on an article that fires no hook never applies.** `host` means something only for an open term of a hook article. The engine does not refuse it elsewhere, for the same reason.
- **Precedence over priority.** A filling that names the host goes before a filling without one, also when the one without a host is of a higher layer. The more specific filling is the one the author wrote for this decision; lex superior and lex posterior rank fillings that are equally specific.

### Choices considered for fillings without a host

The first version of this RFC let only the host law fill a hook's term. With `host` available, the question was whether that restriction should stay for fillings that name no host.

**Keep the restriction.** Only the law of the decision could fill without naming a host, with the hook article's parameters. That would keep the rule that loading one more rulework cannot change another law's decision. But the reason for the restriction was the context: a foreign filling could not know which parameters a chain has. A filling without a host gets no host context either way, so that reason is gone. The restriction would still drop every filling from outside the host law, the policy rule that fills its own hook's term included, and the hook article would give different values under a hook and when called directly.

**Let every regulation fill, under the delegation gate (chosen).** A filling without a host is an ordinary filling of the hook article's open term. This restores what worked before the first version and keeps one rule for open terms, under a hook or not. Restricting who fills is the gate's job, through `delegation_type` on the term.

### The name of the field

`host` continues the vocabulary this RFC already used: the host is the article whose decision or application the hook fires on. It names the same pair as `implements` itself (`law` and `article`) and reads as "this filling belongs to that host". `applies_to` was not a candidate, because a hook already uses it for a filter on the legal character or the submission, not for one article. `origin` and `fired_by` were considered. *Origin* suggests where the filling comes from rather than where it applies. *Fired by* reads as if the host were a hook as well.

### Alternatives Considered

**An override of the output of 4:13.** The host replaces the output of 4:13 with `overrides`. That works, and it stays available. It models a departure (*"in afwijking van"*), however, while paragraph 1 delegates: the special law does not depart from 4:13, 4:13 points at it. The override also replaces the whole output, and the receipt then records a departure where the law describes a delegation.

**Only the host law fills (the first version of this RFC).** Described above. It cannot tell two articles of one law apart, and it drops every filling from outside the host law.

**A slot on the open term naming the case data that goes through.** 4:13 would list, say, `passes: [subsidiejaar]`. That puts the parameters of laws the Awb does not know into the Awb, which reverses the direction that makes inversion of control work.

**Forward all of the host's parameters to every filling.** This removes the `Variable not found` error and drops least privilege with it. A regulation anywhere in the corpus would receive the full case of any decision the hook fires on.

### Implementation Notes

The prototype in this RFC's pull requests builds the decision above. The open questions at the end are not built.

- **The index.** The implements index keeps, for each filling, the host it names, from the newest version of the law, as it keeps the filling itself. `RuleResolver::find_hook_implementations` returns the fillings that name the host and then those that name none; `ImplementationLookup::names_the_host` tells the two apart, so the engine runs each in the scope it belongs to. `find_implementations` returns only fillings without a host.
- **Where the host comes from.** `fire_hooks` records the host in the resolution context while a hook article runs: the articles that fire as hooks on the decision at either hook point, the law and number of the host article, and the parameters it hands its hooks. Afterwards, also after an error, the previous value comes back. Only the open terms of the hook articles read it. The output cache is keyed without the host, so nothing a call computes while a hook runs goes into it.
- **Where the lookup is used.** For resolving the term, for deciding whether a term judged per case is filled (`filled_terms`), for noting the findings of a policy that fills one (`note_policy_findings`), and for the parameters to resolve before the hooks fire. That last one runs before the host's scope is known, so it skips a scoped regulation of another territory that names the host.
- **Defaults read the article's parameters.** A default declares the parameters of the article its term belongs to. An optional parameter the caller left out is then unknown in the default, as it is in the article's own actions ([RFC-036](/rfcs/rfc-036)), instead of a missing variable.
- **The corpus 4:13.** It keeps its hook on the application and has the open term `wettelijke_beslistermijn` (paragraph 1), with paragraph 2 as its default, and an output `uiterste_beslisdatum`. No regulation in the corpus fills the term yet. The only filling in the repository, in the cel construct `beslistermijn`, names its application as the host.
- **Not covered.** `decision_terms`, which lists the open terms a decision may need for a form, does not walk hooks and is unchanged.

Tests in `packages/engine/tests/hook_host_implements.rs` pin the rule on fictional rulework. Two articles of one law fire the same hook, and each gets the filling that names it, with its own parameter. A filling without a host applies to both, to a decision of another law and outside a hook. A filling that names the host goes before one without, and two fillings that name it are ranked by priority. A policy rule that names the host fills the term, and the delegation gate refuses it where the term demands a law. A filling without a host that asks for a parameter of the host fails with `VariableNotFound`. A regulation whose own article fills the term of its own hook article without a host counts, which is the case the first version broke.

## Open questions

### One layer for "wettelijk voorschrift"

`delegation_type` names one regulatory layer. "Bij wettelijk voorschrift bepaald" in 4:13 admits several (a wet, an AMvB, a ministeriële regeling, a verordening) and excludes a beleidsregel. The format cannot say that yet, so the fictional 4:13 in the tests declares no `delegation_type`. Until it can, a term like this one either admits every layer, a policy rule included, or admits only one.

### Policy that belongs to the host

The first version of this RFC asked how the engine could know that a policy rule belongs to the host law, so that it could fill under the hook. With `host` the question no longer decides whether a policy rule can fill: it names the host, and the delegation gate decides whether its layer may. What stays open is whether the format should also say which law a policy rule executes, for instance through a structured `legal_basis`. The engine does not need that for this rule.
